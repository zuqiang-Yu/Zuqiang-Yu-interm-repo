# Working with Vulnerable Populations - Reflection

## Research & Learn

### Who are vulnerable populations, and what challenges might they face in digital spaces?

Vulnerable populations include neurodivergent people (such as people with ADHD or Autism), people with disabilities, people with mental health conditions, older adults, and non-native speakers. In digital spaces, they may struggle with cluttered interfaces, complex language, too many notifications, or designs that assume everyone thinks and works the same way. These barriers can cause frustration and stress, and can make people give up on a tool that was meant to help them.

### What ethical considerations are important when designing for or working with neurodivergent individuals?

Designs should avoid overwhelming users with too many options, flashing animations or constant alerts, and should respect sensory needs such as sensitivity to sound and motion. Communication must be clear and honest, without guilt-tripping language or manipulative tactics such as shaming users for broken streaks. Most importantly, users should feel in control, so features like reminders should be adjustable rather than forced.

### How can we make interactions and content more accessible for people with ADHD or Autism?

We can use simple, direct language and break information into small, manageable steps. Navigation should be predictable, with buttons and menus staying in consistent places and clearly labelled. Reducing cognitive load, for example by showing only what the user needs right now, helps users stay focused instead of feeling overwhelmed.

### How can we support neurodivergent team members in a professional setting?

We can communicate clearly and specifically, avoiding hints, sarcasm or vague requests. Respecting different working styles also matters, as some people prefer written messages while others prefer calls, and some work best at different times of the day. Allowing flexibility and writing down meeting notes and action items helps everyone stay on the same page.

## Reflection

### How can you adjust your communication style to be more inclusive of neurodivergent users and teammates?

I will talk about one thing at a time and make sure it is clear before moving on to the next topic. I will always explain my questions clearly, and I'm happy to communicate by text, in meetings, or face to face, depending on what the other person prefers. If someone replies slowly or is in a different time zone, I will leave a message and wait if it isn't urgent, or ask other colleagues for help if it is. As a non-native English speaker, I know from my own experience how much clear communication matters.

### What are some common UX or communication pitfalls that might make Focus Bear less accessible or supportive?

One pitfall is using emojis as buttons, because users sometimes can't tell that they are clickable. Another is using regional slang or uncommon English expressions, which can confuse non-native speakers. Finally, when reminders appear too often and users don't know how to skip them, the app can actually make it harder to focus, which is the opposite of its purpose.

### What is one practical change you can make in your work to better support vulnerable populations?

When writing code or designing features, I will add text labels or tooltips to buttons instead of relying only on emojis or icons. When writing interface text or documentation, I will use simple, standard English and avoid slang. These small changes make the app easier to understand for neurodivergent users and non-native speakers alike.

## Task

### First-person account: ADHD and productivity tools

I read "5 ADHD Organization Tools That Never Work for Me – and 5 That Do" on ADDitude Magazine (<https://www.additudemag.com/adhd-organization-tools-best-worst/>). The writer, who has ADHD, explained that reminder, list and time-management apps never worked for them, because they kept forgetting they had the apps and how to use them. What did work were simple, visible tools, such as sticky notes right in front of them and external timers to make up for a poor sense of time. This made me realise that Focus Bear's reminders need to be noticeable but not annoying, and the app needs to be easy to use without having to remember how it works.

### One design improvement for Focus Bear

I suggest adding text labels or tooltips to buttons that currently use only emojis. At the moment, users may not realise an emoji is clickable or know what will happen when they tap it. A short label like "Skip break" or "Start focus" removes the guesswork and reduces cognitive load. This connects directly to what I learned from the article, since users with ADHD benefit most from tools that are obvious at a glance.

### Supportive response to a user struggling with executive functioning

**Hypothetical user message:** "I set up all my habits in Focus Bear but I keep ignoring the reminders. I can't even start my morning routine and I feel like a failure."

**My response:**

Hi there, thank you for reaching out and sharing this with us. Struggling to get started is really common, especially for people who find focus and motivation hard, so please don't be too hard on yourself.

One thing that might help is making your morning routine smaller. Try keeping just one or two easy habits, like drinking a glass of water, and add more once that feels manageable. You can also adjust how often reminders appear in your settings so they feel helpful rather than overwhelming.

If you'd like, let me know what your current routine looks like, and I'm happy to help you set up a simpler version step by step. Every small start counts.
