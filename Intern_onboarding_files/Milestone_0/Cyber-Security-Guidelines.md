# Cyber Security Guidelines

## Research & Learn

### 1. Common cyber security threats in a remote work environment

The most common threat in remote work is phishing and social engineering, where attackers pretend to be a colleague, manager or service provider, and remote teams can't easily verify requests in person. Public Wi-Fi in cafés or airports can also be monitored or spoofed, and a reused password leaked from one site can be used to break into other services. In shared homes or public spaces, others may also see your screen or use an unattended computer.

### 2. Best practices for keeping devices and accounts secure

Keep your operating system and apps up to date, turn on full-disk encryption, and set devices to auto-lock after a few minutes of inactivity. Use a unique, strong password for every account stored in a password manager, enable 2FA on all work accounts, and avoid doing work on public Wi-Fi unless you use a VPN or phone hotspot. Only install software from official sources, keep work and personal accounts separate, and never share passwords or 2FA codes with anyone.

### 3. Why lock your computer when away from your desk?

An unlocked computer gives anyone nearby full access to your email, Slack, code, customer data and logged-in accounts – no hacking required. Someone could read confidential information, send messages pretending to be you, install malware or copy data in seconds. Even at home, family members, housemates or visitors could accidentally change or see something sensitive. Locking takes one second.

### 4. How to handle phishing attempts and suspicious links

When something looks suspicious, don't click links, open attachments or reply – instead verify with the sender through a known channel such as a direct Slack message, and type website addresses in yourself. If it is confirmed as suspicious, report it to the team lead or the person responsible for security at Focus Bear, then delete the message. If you've already clicked or entered details, change your password, sign out of all sessions and report it immediately – speed matters more than embarrassment.

### 5. What makes a strong password, and why use a password manager?

A strong password is long (at least 12–16 characters), unique to each site, and avoids predictable content like names, birthdays or `P@ssw0rd` – ideally it is randomly generated or a passphrase of several random words. Nobody can remember dozens of these, so a password manager such as Bitwarden or 1Password generates them, stores them encrypted and only autofills on the correct website, which also helps protect against phishing pages. The master password should be long and used nowhere else, and the password manager itself must have 2FA enabled.

### 6. Why is 2FA important, and when should you enable it?

2FA requires a second proof of identity (something you **have** or **are**) in addition to your password (something you **know**). Even if a password is leaked, phished or guessed, an attacker still can't log in without the second factor. It blocks the vast majority of automated account-takeover attacks.

**Enable it:** on **every account that supports it**, especially email (since it can reset all other accounts), password manager, work tools (Google Workspace, Slack, GitHub, etc.), cloud storage, banking and social media.

## Reflection

### What security measures do I currently follow, and where can I improve?

I currently use face unlock on my phone and computer, have two-factor authentication enabled on my personal accounts, and use strong passwords. Where I can improve is that I have reused some passwords across sites, so I will replace them with unique passwords generated by a password manager.

### How can I make secure behaviour a habit rather than an afterthought?

The most effective approach is to automate security, such as turning on auto-lock, automatic updates and password manager autofill, so I don't have to rely on memory. I will also tie locking my screen to the simple act of standing up, and build a reflex of hovering over any unexpected link before clicking, so the secure way becomes the easiest way.

### What steps will I take to ensure my passwords and accounts are secure?

I will use a password manager for all my work and personal accounts, generate unique passwords of 16+ characters for each one, and replace any passwords flagged as reused or weak. I will enable 2FA on every work account (preferably with an authenticator app), store backup codes in the password manager, and never share passwords or codes.

### What would I do if I suspected a security breach or suspicious activity on my account?

I would first disconnect from the network, then immediately change the password of the affected account (and any account using the same password), sign out of all sessions and check that my 2FA settings haven't been changed. I would then report it straight away to my team lead or the person responsible for security at Focus Bear, explaining what happened, when, and what I've done so far, and keep monitoring the account while following the team's instructions.
